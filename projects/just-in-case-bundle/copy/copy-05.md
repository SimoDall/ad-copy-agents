## COPY (EN)

My sister said something on the phone that I haven't been able to shake.

"If you ended up in the hospital tonight, I could handle it. I've got this."

I believed her. I still do, mostly. She's raised three kids. She's the most capable person I know.

But a week later, totally unrelated, I had a bad flu and asked her to do pickup and bedtime for one night. Nothing dramatic. Just one evening.

By 7:40pm she texted me: "Which stuffed rabbit is the real one? He's lost it."

I laughed. Then I didn't.

Because that's not a medical question. She didn't need to know about allergies or the pediatrician's number — she already had that memorized, honestly. She needed to know something nobody ever thinks to write down: which rabbit. What song. That he won't eat dinner if the plate touches the placemat.

I'd always assumed my "plan B" meant someone capable enough to handle an emergency. Turns out that's the easy part. Everyone knows to call 911.

What breaks a normal evening isn't the crisis. It's the thousand tiny, invisible rules that live only in my head — the ones I follow without even noticing I'm following them.

I'd never actually tested the plan. I'd just never needed to.

That's the part that got me at 2am, lying there replaying her text. How many "normal Tuesdays" had I quietly assumed were covered, just because nothing had gone wrong yet?

So I sat down and wrote it all out — not the emergency stuff, the real stuff. Bedtime steps in order. What sets him off. Which rabbit.

It took one evening. Now it lives in one place, for whoever steps in next.

I didn't buy The Just-In-Case Bundle ($34) because something bad happened. I bought it because something ordinary almost did — and I finally saw the gap for what it was.

If you've got a "plan B" you've never actually tested, it might be worth one evening to find out what it's missing before you need it.
