## Copy (EN)

It was 11:40pm and I was Googling "family emergency binder" with one thumb, lying in a hospital bed, while my mother tried to figure out my daughter's bedtime from a text message.

Three days earlier, none of this was on my mind. Then a routine check-up turned into "we're keeping you overnight," and by the next morning, overnight had turned into four days.

My husband was fine with the big stuff. Insurance card, pediatrician's number, school pickup — he had it.

What he didn't have was the small stuff. Which stuffed rabbit was the real one. That she needs the hallway light on but not the bedroom one. That bedtime falls apart if you skip the song before the teeth-brushing, not after.

He called me eleven times in two days. Not for emergencies. For bedtime.

So that night, from the hospital, I did what every tired, slightly desperate parent does. I searched for a binder, a template, anything. And I found exactly what I expected to find — a wall of "family binders." Except almost every single one was about dying. Estate planning. "If I'm no longer here." Funeral wishes. Will templates stapled to a planner.

I actually laughed, a little, from the hospital bed. I didn't need anyone to know what to do if I died. I needed someone to know what to do tonight.

I'd actually bought one of these a year before — 300-something pages, color-coded, beautifully designed. I filled in maybe six pages and never opened it again. I always assumed that was on me. Too busy, too disorganized.

Lying there at midnight, it clicked: it wasn't me. Every one of those binders was built for the wrong emergency. A will doesn't tell your mother which nightlight to leave on. A funeral plan doesn't explain why the 7pm meltdown isn't actually about being tired.

I hadn't given up on the idea of writing it all down. I'd just only ever found tools built for the day I die, not for an ordinary Tuesday I can't be there.

That's the gap I didn't know I was sitting in — until someone else had to run my house without me, with nothing but a text thread to go on.

I ended up building my own version instead — plain-English pages for the ordinary disruptions (a hospital stay, a long trip, a night you genuinely can't be reached), written for the people stepping in, not for the worst day, for a weird one.

That's The Just-In-Case Bundle — $34. Not a death binder. Just everything whoever's covering for you would actually need to know tonight.

If you've ever shelved a "family binder" because it felt morbid, or abandoned one because it felt endless — it probably wasn't you either.
