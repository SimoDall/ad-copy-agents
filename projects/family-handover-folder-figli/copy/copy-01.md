My dad fell on a Tuesday. Nothing dramatic. A bruised hip, two nights in the hospital.

But I remember sitting in that waiting room, calling his bank to check if a bill had cleared, and the woman on the phone said: "I'm sorry, I can't discuss this account with you."

I had power of attorney. I told her that. She said it didn't matter — she needed account numbers, verification, things only he had in his head.

I hung up and just sat there, in a plastic chair, feeling genuinely stupid. I thought I'd done everything right.

That night I couldn't sleep. I kept Googling "what to do when a parent is hospitalized," landing on forum threads that scared me more than they helped. At 1:50am I gave up and just stared at the ceiling, replaying that phone call.

And that's when it hit me, not from a forum, from my own call. Having the right to act and knowing how to act are two completely different things. A power of attorney tells people you're allowed to decide. It doesn't tell a bank which account, which branch, which password. No lawyer writes that part down for you.

That's the part nobody warns you about. You can do everything "right" and still be standing in a hallway with no actual access.

So I stopped looking for a better document and started asking a different question: who should actually be writing this information down?

Not me. Him. In his own words, on his own afternoon, before anything happens — not because something's wrong, just because it's smart.

That one shift changed everything. It wasn't a warning anymore. It was just... getting ready, together.

My dad filled his out at his kitchen table in under an hour. My sister and I finally know the same things. No more guessing, no more waiting on hold with a stranger who can't tell us anything.

If you've ever had that hallway moment, you already know what I mean.
