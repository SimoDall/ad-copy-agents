I called the bank at 7:40am, still in my pajamas, holding the power of attorney like it was a winning lottery ticket.

"I'm sorry, ma'am, we can't release that information to you."

I actually laughed. Not funny-laughed. The other kind.

My dad had fallen three days earlier. Nothing broken, thank god, but enough to scare both of us straight. And I'd done everything "right" — we'd done the POA two years ago, the will too. I remember feeling almost smug about it at the time. Ahead of the curve.

So when the bank rep said the document "wasn't sufficient on its own," I genuinely didn't understand the sentence.

I sat on my dad's kitchen floor that night, laptop open, googling things like "POA not accepted by bank" at 1am, half expecting to find some rare edge case. Instead I found pages of it. Hundreds of people, same wall.

That's when it clicked, slow and then all at once: the POA gives someone the right to act. It says nothing about where to look. Two completely different problems, and I'd only ever solved one of them.

The second thing I learned came almost by accident. I'd tried, months earlier, handing my dad a printed checklist to fill out. He pushed it back across the table without reading it. "I'm not dying, you know."

So this time I didn't hand him anything. I just left a notebook-style one on the counter, something made for him to write in, his words, his pace, no form to "fill in for" him. Three days later it was half done. He'd written the bank names himself. The passwords. A note about the safe deposit box key.

Nothing about it felt like a warning. It felt like his handwriting.

We're still figuring the rest out. But I'm not on that kitchen floor again.

If any of this sounds familiar — the "we're covered" feeling, then the wall — it's worth looking into before you need to.
