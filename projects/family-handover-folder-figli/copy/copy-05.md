My brother texted me "wait, Dad told YOU that?" and I just stared at my phone for a full minute.

It was 11:40pm. We were comparing notes after a random "hey, just checking in on Dad" call turned into both of us listing what we each thought we knew.

I had the bank. He had the safe deposit box. Neither of us had the password list. Both of us assumed the other one definitely had it covered.

Nobody did.

I didn't sleep great that night. I kept thinking: we talk to Dad all the time. How do two people who talk to the same man every week end up with two different puzzles, both missing half the pieces?

I started typing out a shared doc at 1am so we'd finally be on the same page. Three questions in, I stopped.

I wasn't collecting Dad's information anymore. I was writing my version of it — in my words, on my timeline, from my memory of half a conversation six months ago. If I handed that to Dad to "just confirm," it wouldn't feel like help. It would feel like I'd already decided what mattered and he just had to sign off.

That's when it clicked. The problem was never that Dad wouldn't tell us things. It's that whoever holds the pen decides what the information means — and the moment it's my handwriting instead of his, it stops being his plan and starts looking like my project.

So I scrapped the doc. Instead, I gave him something built for him to fill in himself, on his own afternoon, in his own words, at his own pace. One place. His voice. Same copy for both of us.

Mark and I finally have the same picture. Not because we talked more. Because Dad's the one who wrote the page.

If this sounds familiar, you're not imagining the gap — it's real, and it's fixable before it costs you anything.
